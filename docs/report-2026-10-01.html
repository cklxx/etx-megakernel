ETX on MI300X: one day of measurement

Qwen3-30B-A3B bf16 decode, batch 1 · 2026-10-01 · 22 measured iterations

The claim ETX exists to prove — that the kernels production actually runs, fused into one persistent kernel, beat the same kernels launched one at a time — was false at the start of this day and is true at the end of it. Fusion went from 0.235 ms behind a graph replay of the same tiles to 0.103 ms ahead. Against vLLM the system went from 21% behind to 3.7% behind. Of the eleven changes attempted, three paid; of the twenty-two investigations, eleven ended in a measured negative with an explanation, and those explanations are what moved the work.

1. Machine, protocol, and what may be compared

One box, one protocol, every number re-measured on it. 1× AMD Instinct MI300X VF (SR-IOV virtual function), ROCm 7.14, 20 cores, 235 GB RAM. Qwen3-30B-A3B bf16, greedy, batch 1, 32 generated tokens checked token-for-token against HuggingFace greedy, plus --check-dsl: every DSL operator of the last layer within 2 bf16 ulps of a CPU reference. No performance number in this report comes from a run that failed either gate.

Two cautions that govern every comparison here.
Across machines. The 4.67 ms vLLM figure quoted in earlier reports is from a different, bare-metal machine with a different compiler; our megakernel builds to 15 register spills on this box against 9 there. Absolute milliseconds are not interchangeable between the two. Ratios and the fusion margin are, and both were confirmed on bare metal (§8).
Across timing bases. vLLM's benchmark times host wall clock; ETX reports a device span. These differ by the host work ETX does per step, which was 0.197 ms when first measured. Every headline number in this report is wall clock, the basis vLLM is measured on. Device-span figures are labelled as such.

6. Mechanism findings

The cost of an invalidating acquire, measured two ways

The redesign that flipped the fusion margin rests on one hardware behaviour, and it was measured on both machines with the same result. On the bandwidth side, a phase whose working set is L2-resident takes 0.76 µs without an invalidate and 3.14 µs with one — 4.1×. On the latency side, never used in a design until now, a dependent load chain crossing buffer_inv sc1 pays 1107 ns per hop against 103 ns — 10.6× on bare metal and 11.4× on the VF. Reading activations coherently rather than invalidating and reading cached is 5.8× cheaper. For phases that are dependent chains, the latency figure is the governing one and it is larger.

Occupancy: half the machine's wave slots, and the limit is registers

Measured occupancy is 4.000 of 8 waves per SIMD, identical minimum and maximum across all 1055 dispatches. Both resources bind at once: 512 VGPRs per SIMD divided by 128 per wave is 4, and before the import prune the LDS arena gave the same number. 85.9% of wave-cycles are spent waiting while the memory unit reports zero stalls and LDS bank conflicts are negligible — too little memory-level parallelism, not too little bandwidth. Reaching 6 waves/SIMD needs VGPR ≤ 85 and LDS ≤ 21845 B simultaneously; that is a tile redesign, not a flag.

A hypothesis of ours that the counters refuted. We expected the megakernel to be penalised because its register and LDS budgets are the maximum over all tiles, while separate launches each get their own. The unfused kernel measures VGPR 104 — also 4 waves/SIMD. Occupancy is not part of the fusion penalty. The counters did, however, state the fusion margin directly: mean occupancy per CU is 3.946 fused against 0.974 unfused; the megakernel keeps 98.6% of compute units busy, separate launches about a quarter of that.

Instruction cache

The megakernel is one switch over roughly 400 task types, and the literature names instruction-cache thrashing as an unaddressed cost of this design. Measured here: 99.90% hit rate, 6,958 misses against 6.8 million requests. Ruled out.

7. Two measurement errors we caught

Both were ours, both invalidated analysis we had already acted on, and both were found by checking rather than by a failure.

  • Traces taken during the prompt, not decode. Three rounds of per-phase analysis used traces limited to the first eight steps, which are the teacher-forced prompt with sequence lengths of 1 to 8. vLLM's attention kernel partitions by a hardcoded 256 tokens and exits immediately past the sequence end, so sixteen of twenty attention tasks looked trivial and we concluded the phase had a 4.3× work imbalance. Re-traced in the decode range the spread is 1.17×; there is no imbalance. Timing was never affected — the benchmark only records steps after the prompt — but the attention block was understated by 0.21 ms/token.
  • A statistic that charged imbalance to skew. The budget computed in-phase skew as a phase's span minus its mean body. A phase ends when its last task ends, so that quantity silently includes work imbalance. Recomputed properly, 0.414 ms is start delay and 0.244 ms is imbalance, and the router phase's apparent 4.34 µs of skew was 0.68.

A third error was found in the baseline itself: the streaming benchmark used 256-thread workgroups while the tiles use 1024, so the achievable-rate column was measured at a quarter of the memory parallelism. Corrected, the MoE residual falls from 0.532 to 0.476 ms/token and the router's from 0.164 to 0.046 — most of what we had charged to ETX there was the hardware floor.

9. Batch behaviour, and a premise that was wrong

We expected a megakernel's advantage to grow with batch size, since a persistent kernel pays its scheduling cost once while launch-based systems pay per step. The measurement says the opposite.

BatchvLLM ms/tokenETX ms/tokenRatio
14.8615.2671.08×
22.7933.5981.29×
41.8203.4661.90×
80.941——

ETX does not batch; it replicates. Task counts are exactly linear in request count — 59,978 / 119,956 / 239,912 — because each additional request instantiates another copy of the whole task graph, so every request streams its own copy of the weights. vLLM reads a weight tile once and multiplies it by a B-column activation matrix, which is why its step time barely moves from batch 4 to 8 while its throughput nearly doubles. Interleaving does buy something — batch 4 costs 13.9 ms, not four times 5.27 — but that is latency hiding, not bandwidth sharing, and it cannot beat an approach whose weight traffic is constant in B.

The obvious remedy, a skinny GEMM serving B activation columns from one weight read, was built for the dense phases and lost by 0.29 ms at B=2 for two reasons worth recording: the second column costs 90% of the first, because the tile sits at the point where compute time equals stream time and adding columns doubles the LDS traffic and arithmetic while saving only the weight stream; and a batched phase must wait on every request's previous event, removing the slack that replication provides. LDS also caps the design at B=2 — B=4 would need 82560 B against a 65536 B hardware limit. Batching ETX properly requires matrix instructions for B>1 and whole-layer batching with no per-phase join, including the MoE taking the union of the requests' experts. That is a separate project, not a switch.

10. What vLLM's own fusion is worth

To place our 2.0% on a scale, the same ladder measured for vLLM on this box:

vLLM's graph is worth 6.7×; its operator fusion is worth 0.6%. Host dispatch dominates everything, and both systems have already removed essentially all of it — vLLM through graph capture, ETX through the persistent kernel. On top of that, ETX's megakernel adds 2.0% and vLLM's operator fusion adds 0.6%: the same order of magnitude, and both an order of magnitude below what avoiding per-operator dispatch is worth.

That is worth saying plainly because it is not the thing ETX set out to prove — and because the thing it did set out to prove is now measured, on two machines, with the strong baseline rather than the weak one.
